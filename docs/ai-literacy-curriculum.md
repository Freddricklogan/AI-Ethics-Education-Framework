# AI Literacy Curriculum for Education

## Introduction

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into daily life and the educational experience itself, AI literacy has become a foundational competency for students and educators alike. AI literacy goes beyond knowing how to use AI tools; it encompasses understanding how AI works, recognizing its limitations, evaluating its outputs critically, and engaging thoughtfully with the ethical implications of its deployment.

This curriculum provides a structured framework for teaching AI literacy across educational levels. It is designed to be integrated into existing subject areas rather than treated as a standalone technology course, reflecting the reality that AI intersects with every discipline.

---

## Curriculum Design Principles

1. **Developmentally appropriate**: Content and activities are calibrated to student age and cognitive development
2. **Discipline-integrated**: AI concepts are connected to core subject areas, not isolated
3. **Hands-on and experiential**: Students learn by doing, not just by listening
4. **Critical and ethical**: Technical understanding is paired with ethical reasoning
5. **Inclusive**: Materials and examples represent diverse perspectives and avoid reinforcing stereotypes
6. **Inquiry-driven**: Students are encouraged to ask questions and investigate rather than accept AI outputs uncritically
7. **Educator-empowering**: Teachers are provided with the knowledge and resources to teach AI concepts confidently

## Level 1: Foundations (Elementary / Ages 5-10)

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this level, students should be able to:
- Recognize that some technologies learn from examples (pattern recognition)
- Distinguish between tasks that computers do well and tasks that humans do well
- Understand that AI systems are created by people and reflect human choices
- Describe simple examples of AI in their daily lives
- Express that AI can make mistakes and that human judgment is important

### Unit 1.1: What Is a Smart Machine?

**Duration:** 3-4 class periods (45 minutes each)

**Key Concepts:**
- Computers follow instructions (algorithms)
- Some programs can learn from examples
- AI is not magic -- it is technology built by people

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Sort and Learn*
Students sort objects (colored blocks, pictures of animals, shapes) into categories. Then they examine how a computer might learn to do the same thing by looking at many examples. Students create "training data" by drawing examples and labeling them, then test whether a classmate can use their examples to classify new items.

*Activity 2: Is It AI?*
Present students with a series of technologies (calculator, spell check, voice assistant, thermostat, self-driving car simulation, video game NPC). Students discuss and vote on whether each one "learns" or just "follows rules." Introduce the concept that AI systems can improve by seeing more examples.

*Activity 3: AI in My Day*
Students keep a simple log for one day noting every time they interact with or observe AI (voice assistants, recommendation on a video platform, autocorrect, face filters). Class discussion about how many AI interactions happen without us noticing.

**Assessment:**
Students draw a picture showing one example of AI in their life and write (or dictate) two sentences explaining what the AI does and one thing it might get wrong.

### Unit 1.2: How Computers See Patterns

**Duration:** 3-4 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Pattern recognition is fundamental to AI
- Computers need lots of examples to learn patterns
- More examples generally means better learning
- Different examples lead to different learning

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Train the Teacher*
One student plays the "AI" and leaves the room. The class decides on a secret rule (e.g., "objects that are round"). The "AI" returns and is shown examples one at a time, guessing the rule after each example. Demonstrates how AI learns from examples and how more examples help.

*Activity 2: Rock, Paper, Scissors Predictor*
Students play rock-paper-scissors against a simple online AI that tracks their patterns. Discuss how the AI uses past choices to predict future ones. Explore what happens when students try to be unpredictable.

*Activity 3: Drawing Classifier*
Using Google Quick Draw or similar tool, students draw objects and see if the AI can recognize them. Discuss why some drawings are recognized and others are not. Explore what happens with unusual or creative drawings.

### Unit 1.3: Computers Can Be Wrong

**Duration:** 2-3 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- AI makes mistakes
- Mistakes can affect some people more than others
- It is important to check AI's work
- Humans should make important decisions, not computers alone

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Translation Telephone*
Use an online translation tool to translate a simple sentence through multiple languages and back. Compare the result to the original. Discuss why the AI made mistakes and what kinds of communication would be risky to trust entirely to translation AI.

*Activity 2: The Unfair Robot*
Tell a story about a robot that learns to pick "good apples" but was only trained on red apples. When it encounters green apples, it rejects them all. Discuss fairness: Is the robot being mean? No, it just did not learn about green apples. Who is responsible for fixing it? What does this teach us about AI?

## Level 2: Exploration (Middle School / Ages 11-14)

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this level, students should be able to:
- Explain the basic concepts of machine learning (training data, models, predictions)
- Identify different types of AI applications and how they work at a high level
- Evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, bias, and reliability
- Discuss ethical considerations of AI in specific contexts
- Understand how their data is collected and used by AI systems
- Create simple rule-based or visual programming AI projects

### Unit 2.1: How Machines Learn

**Duration:** 5-6 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Supervised learning: learning from labeled examples
- Features and labels in training data
- Overfitting and underfitting (conceptual)
- The importance of representative training data

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Build a Classifier*
Using Teachable Machine (by Google) or similar platform, students train image classifiers with their own data. They photograph objects from different angles, lighting, and backgrounds. They discover that their model works well on similar images but fails when conditions change, introducing the concept of generalization.

*Activity 2: Data Detective*
Provide students with a dataset (e.g., student survey data about favorite subjects, hobbies, and snack preferences). Students identify patterns manually, then compare their findings to what a simple algorithm discovers. Discuss what patterns are meaningful versus coincidental.

*Activity 3: The Training Data Challenge*
Students are given the task of creating training data for an AI that recommends books to middle schoolers. They discover that if their training data only represents one group's preferences, the AI will not serve everyone well. They iterate on their dataset to make it more representative.

**Assessment:**
Students write a "How It Works" explanation of a machine learning system of their choice (recommendation engine, image recognizer, spam filter), identifying the training data, the pattern being learned, and at least one potential failure mode.

### Unit 2.2: AI and Bias

**Duration:** 4-5 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Bias in training data leads to bias in AI outputs
- Historical bias can be perpetuated by AI systems
- Fairness means different things in different contexts
- Identifying and mitigating bias is an ongoing process

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Biased Bots*
Students examine real-world case studies of AI bias (facial recognition accuracy disparities, biased hiring algorithms, autocomplete suggestions). For each case, they identify: What was the bias? Where did it come from? Who was harmed? What could be done differently?

*Activity 2: Build a Biased Model (and Fix It)*
Students train two image classifiers: one with a biased dataset (e.g., only indoor cats) and one with a balanced dataset. They compare performance and discuss how data representation affects outcomes. Then they improve the biased model by adding more diverse training data.

*Activity 3: Fairness Debate*
Present students with a scenario: A school is using AI to select students for an advanced program. The AI produces results that match historical selection patterns, which underrepresented certain groups. Students debate: Is the AI being fair? What would fair look like? Who gets to decide?

### Unit 2.3: My Data, My Rights

**Duration:** 4-5 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- How personal data is collected by AI systems
- What data is used for and who benefits
- Privacy rights and data protection
- Consent and control over personal data

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Data Footprint Audit*
Students catalog all the data they generate in a typical day: location data, search queries, social media interactions, app usage, school platform logins. They create a visual map of their data footprint and discuss who has access to each type of data.

*Activity 2: Terms of Service Investigation*
Students examine the terms of service and privacy policies of educational tools they use. They identify what data is collected, how it is used, and what rights they have. They rewrite confusing privacy policies in plain language.

*Activity 3: Privacy by Design Challenge*
Students design a fictional app for students (study planner, social network, game) and must decide what data to collect. They create a privacy specification that balances functionality with data minimization. Classmates critique their designs for unnecessary data collection.

### Unit 2.4: Creating with AI

**Duration:** 5-6 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Generative AI capabilities and limitations
- Critical evaluation of AI-generated content
- Appropriate and ethical use of AI tools
- Creativity, authorship, and AI collaboration

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: AI Art Critic*
Students generate images using AI tools (with appropriate safeguards) and traditional art techniques, then compare. They discuss: What is creative about AI art? What is missing? Who is the artist? Can AI be creative?

*Activity 2: Fact or Fiction*
Students use AI to generate informational text about a topic they are studying. They fact-check the AI's output against reliable sources. They document inaccuracies and discuss why AI can produce confident-sounding but incorrect information (hallucination).

*Activity 3: AI Collaboration Guidelines*
Students develop their own classroom guidelines for when and how AI tools should be used in their learning. They debate questions like: When does using AI help learning? When does it replace learning? How should AI assistance be disclosed?

## Level 3: Analysis (High School / Ages 15-18)

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this level, students should be able to:
- Explain how major categories of AI work (machine learning, natural language processing, computer vision, reinforcement learning) at a conceptual level
- Analyze the societal implications of AI deployment in specific domains
- Evaluate AI systems for bias, fairness, and ethical compliance
- Develop informed positions on AI policy questions
- Use AI tools effectively and ethically in academic work
- Understand career implications of AI across industries

### Unit 3.1: Under the Hood

**Duration:** 8-10 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Neural networks and deep learning (conceptual, with optional technical depth)
- Natural language processing and large language models
- Computer vision and convolutional neural networks
- Reinforcement learning
- Limitations of current AI: narrow vs. general intelligence

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Neural Network Simulation*
Students participate in a human neural network exercise where each student represents a node, receiving inputs, applying a simple function, and passing outputs to the next layer. This physical simulation builds intuition for how neural networks process information.

*Activity 2: NLP Deep Dive*
Students examine how language models work by exploring word embeddings (using online visualization tools), studying how context affects prediction, and testing language model capabilities and limitations across different languages, registers, and topics.

*Activity 3: Computer Vision Laboratory*
Using pre-trained models (via web interfaces or simple Python scripts), students explore how computer vision systems interpret images. They test edge cases, adversarial examples, and cultural biases in image recognition.

*Activity 4: Reinforcement Learning Game*
Students play or observe a simple reinforcement learning environment (like a maze-solving agent) and observe how the agent improves through trial and error. They adjust reward functions and observe how different incentives lead to different behaviors.

### Unit 3.2: AI and Society

**Duration:** 8-10 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- AI in healthcare, criminal justice, employment, education, and other domains
- Algorithmic decision-making and due process
- Surveillance and privacy in the age of AI
- Labor market disruption and workforce adaptation
- Environmental impact of AI systems
- Global governance and regulation of AI

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Domain Analysis*
Each student or group selects a domain (healthcare, criminal justice, hiring, lending, education, military, social media). They research how AI is being used, identify benefits and harms, and present a balanced analysis to the class.

*Activity 2: AI Policy Workshop*
Students read excerpts from actual AI policy proposals (EU AI Act, NIST AI Framework, UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics). They draft their own AI policy for their school or community, addressing specific issues they have identified.

*Activity 3: Stakeholder Simulation*
Students participate in a structured role-play where different stakeholder groups (technologists, civil rights advocates, business leaders, educators, students, parents, government regulators) debate a proposed AI deployment. Each group presents their perspective, and the class works toward a resolution.

*Activity 4: Environmental Audit*
Students research the computational resources and energy required to train and run large AI models. They calculate approximate carbon footprints and discuss the environmental trade-offs of AI deployment.

### Unit 3.3: Ethics in Practice

**Duration:** 6-8 class periods

**Key Concepts:**
- Ethical frameworks (utilitarian, deontological, virtue ethics) applied to AI
- Informed consent and autonomy in AI contexts
- Justice and fairness in algorithmic decision-making
- Responsibility and accountability for AI outcomes
- The role of values in technical design choices

**Activities:**

*Activity 1: Ethical Framework Application*
Present students with AI ethical dilemmas (trolley-problem variants for autonomous vehicles, bias-accuracy trade-offs in healthcare AI, privacy-safety trade-offs in surveillance). Students analyze each dilemma using different ethical frameworks and compare conclusions.

*Activity 2: Impact Assessment Practicum*
Students conduct a formal ethical impact assessment of an AI system used in their school or community, using the AI Impact Assessment template. They present findings and recommendations to school administration.

*Activity 3: Values-Sensitive Design*
Students redesign an AI system to better reflect specific ethical values. For example: redesign a social media recommendation algorithm to prioritize wellbeing over engagement, or redesign a predictive policing system to account for historical bias.

## Educator Professional Development

### Module E1: AI Fundamentals for Educators (8 hours)

**Content:**
- What AI is and is not: demystifying the technology
- How common educational AI tools work (adaptive learning, automated grading, predictive analytics, chatbots)
- Hands-on exploration of AI tools available for education
- Understanding AI limitations and failure modes
- Data literacy: what data educational AI collects and how it is used

### Module E2: Teaching AI Literacy (8 hours)

**Content:**
- Curriculum overview and alignment to standards
- Pedagogical approaches for teaching AI concepts
- Adapting activities for different grade levels and subject areas
- Addressing student questions and misconceptions about AI
- Assessment strategies for AI literacy

### Module E3: AI Ethics for Educators (4 hours)

**Content:**
- Ethical principles for AI in education
- Recognizing bias in educational AI tools
- Privacy responsibilities when using AI in the classroom
- Having age-appropriate conversations about AI ethics with students
- Institutional policies and procedures for AI governance

### Module E4: Practical AI Integration (8 hours)

**Content:**
- Evaluating AI tools for educational quality and ethical compliance
- Integrating AI tools into lesson plans effectively
- Maintaining academic integrity in an AI-enabled environment
- Using AI as a teaching tool without replacing teaching
- Developing classroom norms for AI use

## Assessment Framework

### Competency Areas

**Technical Understanding (30%)**
- Can explain how AI systems learn and make predictions
- Understands the role of data in AI performance
- Recognizes different types of AI and their capabilities
- Identifies limitations and failure modes

**Critical Evaluation (25%)**
- Can assess AI outputs for accuracy and reliability
- Identifies potential bias in AI systems and data
- Evaluates claims about AI capabilities skeptically
- Compares AI approaches to alternatives

**Ethical Reasoning (25%)**
- Applies ethical principles to AI scenarios
- Considers multiple stakeholder perspectives
- Understands privacy implications of AI data practices
- Articulates informed positions on AI policy questions

**Practical Application (20%)**
- Uses AI tools effectively and appropriately
- Makes informed decisions about when to use and not use AI
- Communicates about AI clearly to different audiences
- Applies AI literacy across subject areas

### Assessment Methods

- **Portfolio**: Students maintain an AI literacy portfolio throughout the course with artifacts from each unit
- **Case Analysis**: Written analysis of real-world AI scenarios using ethical frameworks
- **Project**: Culminating project applying AI literacy concepts to a domain of student interest
- **Peer Teaching**: Students teach an AI concept to a younger group, demonstrating deep understanding
- **Reflection Journal**: Ongoing reflective writing about AI encounters and evolving understanding
